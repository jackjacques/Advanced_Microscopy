\paragraph{Q1.1} \textit{What would the image of such an object look like on a traditional widefield microscope? What is the minimum numerical aperture that should allow you to resolve the nuclei?}

The image object is on an actual widefild microscope, a bunch of cells that have a certain thikness and absorb a certain amount of light.
The image is then genuinely a phase object:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\Delta z = 0$ (focus):
  A camera or the eye detects only optical intensity $I = \vert{}E\vert{}^2 = 1$. The phase perturbations do not generate amplitude contrast when the object plane is conjugate to the lense (detector).
  \item $\Delta z \neq 0$ (out of contrast):
  As a transparent cell does not absorb light, but has a index of refraction that is a little bit higher than the fluid around, it acts as a small convergent lense, with a gradient index. So when the detector moves of $\Delta z$, the phase changes for the electromagnetic wave, and the focus is lost.
\end{itemize}


For the numerical apperture:
$$R_{\text{nuc}} = 50\,\mu\text{m} \times 0.10 = 5\,\mu\text{m}$$
$$\Leftrightarrow$$
$$D_{\text{nuc}} = 2 \times R_{\text{nuc}} = 10\,\mu\text{m}$$
$$\lambda=550 \mu m$$
We need to compute the diffraction limits.
To resolve the object of a diameter of $D_{nuc}$, we must use two differents metrics:
\begin {itemize}
    \item Abbe Resolution Limit:$$d_{\text{min}} = \frac{\lambda}{2 \cdot NA} \le D_{\text{nuc}} \implies NA \ge \frac{\lambda}{2 D_{\text{nuc}}}$$ $$\Leftrightarrow$$ $$NA \ge \frac{0.55\,\mu\text{m}}{2 \times 10\,\mu\text{m}} = \mathbf{0.0275}$$
    \item Rayleigh Criterion:$$r_{\text{Rayleigh}} = 0.61 \frac{\lambda}{NA} \le D_{\text{nuc}} \implies NA \ge 0.61 \frac{\lambda}{D_{\text{nuc}}}$$ $$\Leftrightarrow$$ $$NA \ge 0.61 \times \frac{0.55\,\mu\text{m}}{10\,\mu\text{m}} \approx \mathbf{0.034}$$
\end{itemize}
So, the absolute theoretical lower bound to resolve the nucleus as an individual feature is $NA \approx 0.034$.

\paragraph{Q1.2} \textit{What kind of technique could be used to retrieve information from a sample that is mostly transparent?}

There is two electromagnetic waves that goes through the sample: 
\begin{itemize}
  \item the direct one, that is not diffracted and has no phase lag;
  \item The one that goes across the cell and is diffracted by the nucleus ot the borders of the cell.
\end{itemize}
Thoses waves are in phase opposition. 
So a solution could be to set a darkfield: as dust particules lighten by the sun rays in a dark room, we can see very small dust particules that we would never without the darkfield.
They are only visible because the diffraction of the light.
This parallel is true in microscopy:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We select only a cone of inclined light.
  \item We assert $NA_{illum} > NA_{obj}$: the angle of the light beam is superior to the objective aperture angle.
  \item When the light crosses a cell, the borders and organs diffract the light in evry directions.
\end{itemize}
Result: the background is dark, the cytoplasma is dark too (does not deflect light beams) but only the border is grey.

\paragraph{Q3.2} \textit{Intuitively, how come the process of imaging through an optical system involves
Fourier transforms?}

The light that goes through an object decomposes into a sum of plane waves. The angle of deflection then directly depends on the fine details. Indeed, the object's spatial frequencies are given by: $\sin\theta = \lambda f_x$. The lense focuses each wave $\Sigma_\theta$ into a single point $x_f, y_f$, in the BFP. This plane thus physically realizes a 2D spatial Fourier transform of the incident wave field. 
The second lens then collects these focal points and refocuses them onto the image plane, which performs the inverse operation (inverse Fourier transform-ifft/ifft2) of the recombined plane waves.

\paragraph{Q3.3} \textit{A target can be used to quantify the resolution of a microscope. Use the different
targets provided to try and assess the resolution of your system. Explain the method you
are are using as well as your results.}

To calibrate optical resolution experimentally, a standard negative USAF 1951 resolution target is imaged through the pipeline for each numerical aperture:

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/part2_Resolution_calibration_USAF1951.png}
  \caption{Simulation of the USAF 1951 resolution target across $NA \in \{0.01, 0.1, 1.0\}$, comparing global field views, central zoomed regions, and Michelson contrast modulation.}
  \label{fig:part2_usaf}

\textbf{Methodology:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The target image is rescaled to physical coordinates ($L = 1.2\text{ mm}$, $N = 1024$), transformed to frequency space via \texttt{fft2}, filtered with the binary pupil mask, and back-transformed using \texttt{ifft2}.
    \item A 1D intensity cross-section is extracted across the periodic bars in the central region of interest.
    \item The contrast is evaluated across periodic elements:
    \[
    C = \frac{I_{\max} - I_{\min}}{I_{\max} + I_{\min}}
    \]
    According to the Rayleigh criterion, two adjacent bars are considered resolved when the intensity dip between peaks is at least $19\%$ to $26\%$ ($C \ge 0.15\text{--}0.20$).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Observations}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item When $NA = 0.01$, the thick bars merges, the contrast decreases;
  \item when $NA = 0.1$, the smallest element of the target we can see is the group 1, element 1. So, as : $Resolution = 2^{\left( \text{group number}+\frac{\text{element number}-1}{6}\right)}= 2.00 lp/mm$ in line per milimeter. $d = \frac{1}{f} \times 1000 \quad (\mu\text{m})$, so $d =  \mathbf{500\,\mu\text{m}}$.
  \item when $NA = 1.0$, the smallest element of the target we can see is the group 1, element 2. So, as : $Resolution\approx 2.24 lp/mm$ in line per milimeter. So $d\approx \mathbf{445\,\mu\text{m}}$.
\end{enumerate}

The natural choice for NA is then $NA = 0.1$
As the nucleus radius is about $10 \mu m$, thus, it is never discernable. 

\section{Part 3 - Imaging Contrast Mechanisms}

\paragraph{Q.4}

In brightfield, the unscattered light dominates and the transparent cells are barely visible (only the absorbing 
nuclei and a faint edge). In darkfield, the annular pupil blocks the unscattered (zero-frequency) 
light: the background becomes black and only light scattered by the cells reaches the camera, so the cells appear bright, 
mostly at their edges where the phase gradient (light deflection) is strongest. 
Darkfield reveals the cell outlines but does not give a quantitative phase measurement.

\paragraph{Q5} \textit{Phase contrast (weak object transfer function).}

For a weak phase object ($\varphi \ll 1$), the field can be linearized:
\[
E = e^{i\varphi} \approx 1 + i\varphi .
\]
The unscattered component (1) and the scattered component ($i\varphi$) are in quadrature,
so $|1 + i\varphi|^2 = 1 + \varphi^2 \approx 1$: the cells are invisible. A Zernike phase plate
multiplies the zero frequency by $i$ (quarter-wave shift), giving
\[
E' = i + i\varphi = i(1+\varphi) \quad \Rightarrow \quad I = (1+\varphi)^2 \approx 1 + 2\varphi ,
\]
so the phase is converted linearly into intensity. Since our cells reach $\varphi \approx 30$ rad,
far from the weak-object regime, the phase was rescaled to $\varphi_{\max} = 0.3$ rad to stay
within the validity of the linearization. In brightfield, this weak object is only visible
through its absorption (panel 3), whereas in phase contrast (panel 4) the cells appear as
bright disks whose brightness follows the phase map.

\section{Part 4 - Detector Sampling and Noise}

\paragraph{Q6}\textit{Simulate the same object with two cameras: Pixel pitch 1 μm, SNR=30 and 3.5 μm, SNR=60
and explain how both parameters affect your ability to resolve the objects.}

The function \texttt{addCameraEffects} models the two main limitations of a real detector:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Spatial sampling:} each pixel integrates the intensity over its surface. The simulation grid is resampled with \texttt{cv2.resize} (option \texttt{INTER\_AREA}, which averages over the pixel area), using the pixel size projected in the object plane, $p_{\text{pitch}}/M$.
  \item \textbf{Photon noise:} the photon count follows a Poisson law, so $\text{SNR} = N/\sqrt{N} = \sqrt{N}$. The image is normalized so that its maximum equals $N_{\max} = \text{SNR}^2$ photons ($900$ for SNR=30, $3600$ for SNR=60), then drawn with a Poisson generator.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/part4_camera.png}
  \caption{Ideal image (NA=0.5), camera A (1 $\mu$m, SNR=30) and camera B (3.5 $\mu$m, SNR=60). Top: full field. Bottom: zoom on one nucleus.}
  \label{fig:part4_cameras}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Effect of the pixel pitch.} The pixel pitch sets the sampling frequency of the image. The Nyquist criterion requires at least 2 pixels per period of the smallest feature. A nucleus has a diameter of $10\,\mu$m, which is about $10$ pixels for camera A but only about $3$ pixels for camera B. Camera B is therefore barely above the limit: the nucleus is detected, but its shape is blocky and its contrast is diluted by the averaging over large pixels. Camera A samples it correctly. For NA=0.5, the optical Nyquist limit is $\lambda/(4\,NA)\approx 0.28\,\mu$m: both cameras are far above it, so the detector, not the objective, limits the resolution.

\textbf{Effect of the SNR.} Photon noise has a relative amplitude $1/\text{SNR}$ ($3.3\%$ for A, $1.7\%$ for B). It hides details whose contrast is below this level. The nuclei (transmission $0.6$) and the cells (transmission $0.85$) have a contrast well above $3.3\%$, so the noise appears as grain but does not hide them. \textbf{Trade-off.} A larger pixel collects more photons (hence a higher SNR) but samples the image more coarsely: this is the fundamental trade-off between spatial resolution and sensitivity.

\textbf{Conclusion.} Camera A resolves the nuclei better: sampling is the limiting factor here, and the nuclei are contrasted enough to tolerate SNR=30. Camera B would only be preferable for very low-contrast structures with larger details.

\section{Part 5 - Defocus and Depth of Field}

\paragraph{Q8} \textit{Explain your reasoning behind how you constructed this simulation. Show how both layers contribute to the image.}

\textbf{Construction.} Each layer is modeled as a thin object with complex transmission $t_j(x,y)=\sqrt{I_j}\,e^{i\varphi_j}$. A plane wave crosses the layers one after the other:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the field right after layer 1 is $t_1$;
  \item it propagates freely over $\Delta z$ up to layer 2 (angular spectrum method);
  \item layer 2 \textbf{multiplies} the field by its transmission $t_2$.
\end{enumerate}
\[
E_{\text{tot}} = t_2 \cdot \mathcal{P}_{\Delta z}\{t_1\}
\]
The layers are multiplied and not added: light goes through the first layer and then through the second one, so their phases add up, whereas an addition of fields would double the background intensity and have no physical meaning. The second layer is an independent random object generated on the same grid. The resulting field is then filtered by the pupil (NA=0.5) like in the previous parts.

\textbf{Contribution of the layers.} The camera is conjugated to one plane only. Imaging $E_{\text{tot}}$ directly focuses on layer 2; to focus on layer 1, the field is back-propagated by $-\Delta z$ before the pupil filtering.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/part5_two_layers.png}
  \caption{Two-layer object ($\Delta z = 20\,\mu$m, NA=0.5). Left: focus on layer 2. Right: focus on layer 1.}
  \label{fig:part5_two_layers}
\end{figure}

The depth of field is approximately $\lambda/NA^2 \approx 2.2\,\mu$m for NA=0.5, much smaller than $\Delta z = 20\,\mu$m. In each image, the layer in focus is sharp (nuclei and cell edges visible), while the other one is far out of the depth of field: it only contributes a blurred background with diffraction fringes, which lowers the contrast of the sharp layer. [À adapter à vos images : décrire ce que vous voyez réellement.] Both layers therefore contribute to the image, but only the one in focus provides usable detail. This is the principle of optical sectioning limitation in widefield microscopy: out-of-focus planes are never removed and degrade the image.
