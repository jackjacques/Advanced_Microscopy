\section*{Introduction}

The goal of this lab is to simulate the formation of an image in an optical microscope, step by step: the object, the objective, the contrast techniques, the camera and the defocus. We used Python (NumPy, Matplotlib, OpenCV) instead of MATLAB, with the same functions (\texttt{fft2}, \texttt{fftshift}, \texttt{ifft2}). All simulations use $\lambda = 550$ nm and a grid of $4096 \times 4096$ pixels over a 1 mm field of view (pixel of $0.24\,\mu$m). We first used a coarser grid, but then the pupils for large NA did not fit in the grid.

\section{Part 1 -- Defining the Object}

\paragraph{Q1} We wrote \texttt{makeObject} following the template given by the professor, with the parameters of the lab (10 cells of radius $50 \pm 10\,\mu$m, nucleus radius $= 0.1 \times$ cell radius, $\Delta n = 0.02$). Each cell is a sphere: its thickness gives the phase map. The nucleus absorbs a little light (amplitude 0.95), which gives the intensity map.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/part1_Intensity_and_Phase_maps.png}
  \caption{Intensity map (left) and phase map (right) of the object.}
  \label{fig:part1_object}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Q1.1} \textit{What would the image of such an object look like on a traditional widefield microscope? What is the minimum numerical aperture that should allow you to resolve the nuclei?}

The cells are almost transparent: they mostly change the phase of the light, and a camera only sees the intensity. So in a widefield microscope the image would be almost uniform, with only the nuclei appearing as small, slightly dark spots.

The nucleus has a diameter $D = 2 \times 0.1 \times 50 = 10\,\mu$m. With the Rayleigh criterion:
\[
0.61\,\frac{\lambda}{NA} \le D \quad\Rightarrow\quad NA \ge 0.61 \times \frac{0.55}{10} \approx 0.034
\]
So we need at least $NA \approx 0.03$ to resolve the nuclei.

\paragraph{Q1.2} \textit{What kind of technique could be used to retrieve information from a sample that is mostly transparent?}

We need a technique that turns the phase into intensity:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{darkfield}: the direct light is blocked and only the light deflected by the cells is collected, so the cells appear bright on a black background;
  \item \textbf{phase contrast} (Zernike): a phase plate shifts the direct light so that the phase differences become intensity differences;
  \item \textbf{DIC} (differential interference contrast), or simply a slight defocus.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Q3} \textit{Simulate imaging of the object for NA = 0.01, 0.1, 1.0. Compare the resulting images. Are the cells resolved? The nuclei?}

The field is Fourier transformed (\texttt{fft2}, \texttt{fftshift}), multiplied by the pupil, transformed back (\texttt{ifft2}), and the image is $|E|^2$.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/part2_cells_vs_NA.png}
  \caption{Image of the object for NA = 0.01, 0.1 and 1.0.}
  \label{fig:part2_cells_na}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{NA = 0.01}: the image is very blurred. The cells appear as dark spots with rings around them and the nuclei are not resolved (resolution $\approx 34\,\mu$m, larger than the nucleus).
  \item \textbf{NA = 0.1}: the cells are well separated, with a dark edge and rings inside, and the nuclei are visible (resolution $\approx 3.4\,\mu$m).
  \item \textbf{NA = 1.0}: almost all the light goes through, so the image looks like the real object: the nuclei are sharp but the cells become almost invisible.
\end{itemize}
A larger NA gives a better resolution, but it does not make the transparent cells more visible.

\paragraph{Q3.1} \textit{Where is the pupil of the objective physically located?}

The pupil is in the back focal plane of the objective. All the rays leaving the sample with the same angle meet at the same point of this plane, so an aperture there limits the angles collected by the objective.

\paragraph{Q3.2} \textit{Intuitively, how come the process of imaging through an optical system involves Fourier transforms?}

The light leaving the object can be seen as a sum of plane waves with different angles: the fine details send light at large angles and the large details at small angles. The objective focuses each angle to one point of its back focal plane, so this plane contains the Fourier transform of the object. The second lens recombines these waves on the camera, which is the inverse Fourier transform.

\paragraph{Q3.3} \textit{Use the different targets provided to try and assess the resolution of your system. Explain the method you are using as well as your results.}

The provided USAF image is small (about $250 \times 250$ pixels) and has no physical size, so its bars were too large to test our microscope. We therefore drew the USAF 1951 target ourselves at its real size (groups 4 to 9, bars from $31\,\mu$m down to $0.55\,\mu$m), imaged it for each NA, and looked for the smallest group of bars that we can still separate.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/part2_Resolution_calibration_USAF1951.png}
  \caption{USAF 1951 target imaged with NA = 0.01, 0.1 and 1.0. Bottom: zoom on the small groups.}
  \label{fig:part2_usaf}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{NA = 0.01}: only the largest bars are separated, up to group 4 element 3 (bars of $25\,\mu$m).
  \item \textbf{NA = 0.1}: the bars are separated up to group 7 element 5 (bars of $2.5\,\mu$m).
  \item \textbf{NA = 1.0}: all the bars of the target are separated, even group 9 (bars of $0.55\,\mu$m), so the resolution is better than what the target can measure.
\end{itemize}
The resolution improves about 10 times when the NA is 10 times larger, as expected.

\section{Part 3 -- Imaging Contrast Mechanisms}

\paragraph{Q4} \textit{Set NA = 0.5. Modify the pupil to simulate darkfield imaging (annular pupil). Compare the image to brightfield.}

For darkfield, we block the center of the pupil and keep a ring between NA = 0.05 and 0.5. In brightfield, the cells are hardly visible (only a thin edge and the nuclei). In darkfield, the background is black and the edges of the cells appear bright, because only the light deflected by the cells reaches the camera.

\paragraph{Q5} \textit{Modify the pupil to simulate a phase contrast microscope.}

For phase contrast, we multiply the center of the pupil (the direct light) by $i$, which is a quarter-wave phase shift. This method only works for a weak phase ($e^{i\varphi} \approx 1 + i\varphi$), but our cells reach about 30 rad. We therefore reduced the phase of the object to 0.3 rad. The cells then appear as bright disks, brighter where they are thicker.

\section{Part 4 -- Detector Sampling and Noise}

\paragraph{Q6} \textit{Write a function addCameraEffects. Simulate the same object with two cameras (pixel 1 $\mu$m, SNR = 30 and pixel 3.5 $\mu$m, SNR = 60) and explain how both parameters affect your ability to resolve the objects.}

\texttt{addCameraEffects} first resamples the image to the size of the camera pixels (\texttt{cv2.resize}, which averages the light over each pixel). Then it adds Poisson (photon) noise: for an SNR of 30, the background receives $30^2 = 900$ photons per pixel.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/part4_camera.png}
  \caption{Ideal image (NA = 0.5) and the two cameras. Bottom: zoom on one nucleus.}
  \label{fig:part4_cameras}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Pixel size}: a nucleus ($10\,\mu$m) covers about 10 pixels with the 1 $\mu$m camera, but only about 3 pixels with the 3.5 $\mu$m camera. With the large pixels, the nucleus becomes a small square block and we lose its shape.
  \item \textbf{SNR}: the noise is about $1/\text{SNR}$, so 3\% for the first camera and 2\% for the second one. The nucleus only darkens the image by about 10\%, so the noise is visible: the first image is more grainy.
\end{itemize}
The first camera gives the best image of the nuclei: it is noisier, but it keeps their shape. A bigger pixel collects more light (higher SNR) but gives a lower resolution.

\section{Part 5 -- Defocus and Depth of Field}

\paragraph{Q7} \textit{Implement a fresnelPropagate function using the angular spectrum method to simulate a 20 $\mu$m object defocus.}

With the angular spectrum method, we take the Fourier transform of the field, multiply each frequency by a phase term that depends on the propagation distance, and take the inverse Fourier transform. The defocused field is then imaged with NA = 0.5.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/part5_defocus.png}
  \caption{Object in focus (left) and defocused by 20 $\mu$m (right).}
  \label{fig:part5_defocus}
\end{figure}

In focus, the cells are almost invisible. With 20 $\mu$m of defocus, the nuclei become blurred, but the cells become clearly visible with a dark edge: the defocus turns the phase into intensity. The depth of field at NA = 0.5 is only about $2\,\mu$m, so 20 $\mu$m is far out of focus.

\paragraph{Q8} \textit{Extend the simulation to a two-layer object. Explain your reasoning behind how you constructed this simulation. Show how both layers contribute to the image.}

The light goes through the first layer, propagates over 20 $\mu$m, then goes through the second layer. So we multiply the field of layer 1 (after propagation) by the transmission of layer 2:
\[
E = t_2 \times \text{propagate}(t_1,\ 20\,\mu\text{m})
\]
The two layers are multiplied and not added, because the light crosses them one after the other. The second layer is another random object. To focus on layer 2 we image $E$ directly; to focus on layer 1 we propagate $E$ back by $-20\,\mu$m before the pupil.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/part5_two_layers.png}
  \caption{Two-layer object: focus on layer 1 (left) and on layer 2 (right).}
  \label{fig:part5_two_layers}
\end{figure}

Both layers are visible in each image. The layer in focus has sharp nuclei but almost invisible cells, while the other layer is out of focus: its cells appear with dark or bright edges and its nuclei are blurred. A widefield microscope cannot remove the out-of-focus layer, it always adds to the image.

\section*{Conclusion}

We built a complete virtual microscope, from the object to the camera image. We saw that the NA sets the resolution, that transparent cells need a contrast technique (darkfield, phase contrast) or a defocus to be visible, and that the camera pixel size and noise also limit the final image.
